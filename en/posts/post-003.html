Spring Is Daybreak, Summer Is Monsters.

Published 2025.12.22

#Essay #AboutMe #Yokai #KeepingMemories #LoveHotel #MitsumineShrine #Ushiuchibo

When the tall building in front of Kichijoji Station got torn down, an elaborately decorated love hotel peeked out from behind it. It's now clearly visible even from the main street with the movie theater and the supermarket, which might be the kind of thing that makes a mother with kids in tow want to cover their eyes. And yet the love hotel just stood there, with the expression of a quiet art-club kid getting recognized at a school assembly — as if to say, "I'm just doing my job here" — standing tall, while secretly wishing it could avoid all this attention.

That day, I'd headed out full of purpose to buy "The Complete Cocktail Compendium" for work reasons, and ended up mistakenly buying Shigeru Mizuki's "Encyclopedia of Japanese Yokai" instead. As Sei Shonagon famously put it, spring is for dawn, summer is for night, autumn is for dusk, winter is for early morning, and so on — but night is precisely where yokai come into their own, which is to say, I'd like to propose that "summer is for monsters." Since I ended up picking this book up by some kind of fate, I find myself wanting to study up thoroughly on yokai ahead of the coming summer of 2026.

When studying yokai, I'd suggest doing what you'd do studying whisky: start by finding the ones you personally like. Memorizing all the details — that single-malt whisky is made from 100% malted barley and must come from a single distillery, that this corresponds to, say, Nikka's "Yoichi" or "Miyagikyo" — is a real slog. But if you can just decide that "Taketsuru" tastes good, things move a lot faster.

In the world of yokai, the equivalent of "Taketsuru" is Ushiuchibo. I fell for Ushiuchibo the moment I laid eyes on it.

Ushiuchibo is a yokai that has long appeared in Tokushima Prefecture, sneaking into cattle sheds night after night to drink cows' blood and kill them. No one has ever actually seen its true form; depending on the source, it's said to be a black tanuki, or a shadow-like creature with no real substance at all.

One notable thing about Ushiuchibo is that no physical countermeasure works on it whatsoever. Even the mountain-sized giant Onyudo (whose true identity is actually an otter) can be brought down by an arrow to the leg, but against Ushiuchibo, all such attacks are entirely useless.

Because of how vicious it was, villages held a ritual festival to drive Ushiuchibo away, burning money, bamboo, and straw as offerings — and it's said that any household that donated too little of the burnable material would, after the festival, have its ashes thrown at their house, and that any livestock belonging to a household hit with these ashes was believed to soon collapse and die. In other words, fear of Ushiuchibo took on a life of its own within the community and ended up causing very real, human-made disasters — which is genuinely terrifying.

For what it's worth, another livestock-killing yokai is a wolf-like creature called "Shii," but in any case, if yokai are metaphors for that kind of fear, then we can begin to grasp just how serious it was for people back then to have their cattle or horses killed — presumably from the standpoint of both transport and securing food.

＊　＊　＊

An anomaly like a love hotel suddenly showing its face at a station front bustling with students and families — that is, without question, the work of a yokai. The work of "Love-Hotel Uchidashi-bo," I hereby declare — and if my simply naming it this way is enough to get this desk-invented creature into common circulation, and it someday takes on a physical form of its own, that would be a truly wonderful thing. I ought to be a little more grateful that I get to enjoy my days with a head this thoroughly bewitched.

With all that in mind, I thought I might pay a visit to an Inari shrine for the first time in a while, but then I remembered that I'd once gone out of my way, with unusual conviction, to visit Mitsumine Shrine (which enshrines wolves, and is said to get displeased — with bad consequences — if you go "cheat" on it with another animal), so apparently that's off the table now.
