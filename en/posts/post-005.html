Year's End. A Pomeranian Facing Down the Wind Really Exists, and Tonkotsu Ramen Blows Out White Under the Lights.

Published 2025.12.29

#Essay #AboutMe #YearInReview #Pomeranian #IekeiRamen

At 1 a.m. on the last stretch of the year, the only people in the Iekei ramen shop are men with hollow eyes. They cram into the counter seats arranged in a U-shape, slurping their noodles with total single-mindedness, all wearing the air of "I'm still fine" even though they clearly stopped being fine a while ago. In the passage behind the tall wall — the inside of the U — Vietnamese or Filipino women staff go back and forth serving food. Depending how you look at it, it could almost pass for some kind of séance. The men chant koi-me-kata-me-oo-me [firm noodles, extra fatty broth, lots of garlic]. Is this a sacred prayer, or a curse? As for me, I say suku-name-ato-zenbu-futsuu [less noodles, everything else normal]. My eyes haven't glazed over yet. I should still be fine.

In the basement of a mixed-use building, where only the sound of slurped noodles and the clatter of metal from the kitchen stand out, the ceiling lights are oddly bright, and the tonkotsu ramen I'd ordered blew out pure white in their reflection — almost divine.

Suddenly, a man sitting on the other side of the wall stands up and shouts, "Garlic!" His voice was pale-blue, like peach fuzz, and his eyes were fixed on some empty point in midair. The staff respond politely with, "One moment, please." I found myself wondering whether they were, in fact, properly annoyed at this guy underneath it all, even if it didn't show in their expression or tone. I hope his arrogance came through as arrogance even across the language and culture barrier, and I like to imagine them, after their shift, saying something in the back like, "let's ban that jerk from earlier." Or maybe they've just gotten completely used to customers like that, but I really hope no one takes this as a normal, everyday thing in Japan. For what it's worth, I don't add garlic even when the next day's a day off. So I should still be fine.

After the staff handed the garlic guy his order, the good-looking, mushroom-cut young man with the subtle features sitting two seats down from me said, "Garlic, please," in a voice and expression that could've come straight out of pillow talk. Not that there's anything wrong with that — he ordered it perfectly reasonably, and it's probably just that, next to the earlier guy's rudeness, his own manner looked relatively exaggerated by comparison. My hunch that he probably kicks telephone poles when he's alone is undoubtedly just my own unfair bias. But there was no helping the fact that his eyes, too, were clouded with alcohol, staring off at some empty point in midair.

Still, no matter what you look like, ordering garlic right after that first rude guy must have taken some nerve. But he did restore this place back to its normal shape, and I'm sure everyone here is grateful to him for it.

＊　＊　＊

I leave the shop. The darkness of a 2 a.m. night surely won't get any deeper than this. Watching the dying flicker of my turbo lighter, I suddenly remember a Pomeranian defying the wind. At almost the same moment, I felt the smell of year's end rising up together with the blue smoke of my cigarette.

I'd seen the Pomeranian facing down a strong wind a few days earlier, in the middle of carrying a huge amount of bread for work. But the idea of bread being heavy is something that, in my day-to-day life, I find hard to accept. I can forgive a stack of copy paper being heavy; I can imagine the weight of a wallet stuffed with too much loose change. But that a single, feather-light slice of bread should somehow become heavy in bulk — that one, I really struggle with...

While I was pondering that, I came face to face with a well-bred-looking Pomeranian, a red bandana around its neck, tethered to a leash trailing from a woman's hand, its expression grim as it faced down a strong wind. Laundry would dry nicely on a day like this, and it might be perfect for flying a kite, but I had a feeling it was going to keep being a rough day for that Pomeranian. There's a saying that when the wind blows, the bucket-maker profits — but with absolutely no connection to any of that, the Pomeranian was simply facing down the strong wind. The Pomeranian knows nothing about bucket-makers, and for that matter, knows almost nothing about the world at all.

The same goes for me. Even while getting buffeted by the wind, I need to think about the kinds of things a bucket-maker would think about — that is, about business, about what's actually happening out there in the wide, distant world. In other words, I need to aim to become a Pomeranian who actually knows about the world. Looking back, 2025 felt like it was full of things that had nothing whatsoever to do with me, so for 2026 I find myself wanting to behave in a way that brings those real, actual things a little closer to home. Which means, first of all, I need to fix the fact that I can't manage to put my trash out on the right day.
