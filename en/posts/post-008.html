Pouring Strawberry Syrup on Leftover Snow and Eating It.

Published 2026.02.08

#AboutMe #BlissfulSweets #Danchi #Phobia #Cornelius #Vaporwave

A certain day in February. A clear day with snow still lingering on the ground. (Left, Right, Flat, Point…) Cornelius's "Point of View Point" is playing quietly in my ears. (View………) Out on a walk, I'm having trouble telling the difference between snow and de-icing agent. A gray danchi housing complex appears up ahead. (Point.)

The windows, balconies, railings, and outdoor AC units, all arranged at even intervals, are as uniform as an Excel copy-and-paste — or rather, like values auto-filled by dragging a fill handle down a column. In truth, look closely and there are slight differences between them (whether or not there are plants on the balcony, the color of the curtains, that sort of thing) — but even so, it's still almost the same thing, built into the same slots, over and over. And oddly enough, the more of those minor differences — or things you might call originality — there are, the colder it somehow feels; and the more the pleasant light of a clear sky shines on the building, the more it stirs up a feeling of emptiness, too.

No individuality or deviation can ever escape the danchi's standard specifications; it just becomes paint inside a picture frame. It closely resembles the popular kid from the countryside who's never known the city, or the self built up through job-hunting self-reflection, or an overprotected mother's child — all of them wearing that same, oddly satisfied expression. A gas meter half-buried in snow shows an entirely unremarkable reading.

＊　＊　＊

Somewhere in the same family as megalophobia (fear of huge objects) or thalassophobia (fear of the ocean), there exists a fear of danchi housing complexes. Call it danchi-phobia, if you like. In English, danchi would be something like "public housing" or a "housing complex," but—

A danchi is a type of collective housing complex built in Japan mainly during the period of rapid postwar economic growth, made up of multiple residential buildings arranged on a single site, constructed under a coordinated plan to address the housing shortage in urban areas. — written by chat GPT

I get the feeling that the very origin story of the danchi described above is exactly what breeds the sense of dread hanging over it, and if there's ever a chance to export the concept of danchi-phobia overseas, I'd still want to keep the word "danchi" untranslated and call it "danchi-phobia."

I get goosebumps just looking at a danchi, so I suppose I'm danchi-phobic, but hiding inside that is also a perverse fascination — the kind where you're staring intently through the gaps between the fingers of a hand covering your eyes. It's the same psychology as an elementary schooler who wants to touch mud, or the compulsion to keep endlessly watching dashcam footage, or Nobita finally arriving at Shizuka's bath — which is to say, well, it's not really a bad thing after all.

Anyway, when someone says "the age of rapid economic growth" or "mass production," what comes to mind for me is that pink background with the plaster bust, palm trees, an early Windows logo, and shopping malls. Vaporwave, though supposedly dead, still lives on inside me, and I feel that the same nostalgia and reverence underlying Vaporwave lives equally inside the danchi.

＊　＊　＊

Spotify's autoplay keeps picking songs so arbitrarily it's like drawing lots. Cornelius → 電ǂ鯨 → 猫シCorp. → BUMP OF CHICKEN → et cetera. But then, in the first place, the box only has songs I like inside it, so there's no real problem. I try not to think there's a problem with there being no problem.

The places where snow lingers are the places with no risk of being trodden on, places with poor heat conduction. I want to pour strawberry syrup over the fresh snow found in a spot like that and eat it, just for a moment's satisfaction. That's what I used to do as an elementary schooler. This too is a form of nostalgia — equal, once again, to paint inside a picture frame.
