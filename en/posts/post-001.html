You Shouldn't Answer Your Year-End Tax Adjustment Like It's an MBTI Quiz.

Published 2025.12.18

#Essay #Poetry #AboutMe #MBTI #YearEnd #TaxAdjustment #SakutaroHagiwara

It's a lot like scooping goldfish with the same mindset you'd use to scoop super balls, or missing a country train because you're still running on city-time logic — that is to say, if you go into it lightly, you'll pay for it later.

For what it's worth, I filled out my year-end tax adjustment completely seriously. Sure, it took about as long as an MBTI quiz, but I did it properly.

No spouse, no dependents — with every "none of the above" I clicked, the number of documents and fields I had to fill in kept shrinking in front of my eyes, and it made me a little sad. This is supposed to be a fairly involved task, but for lack of anything to declare, it just kept getting easier. It's like the tree in an early Pokémon town that needs Cut, or the boulder that needs Strength — or a Taiko no Tatsujin chart with a branching section. For now, with my current stats, all I can do is walk on by.

So while I'm grateful there's less to fill in, I also get the feeling I can't stay like this forever.

Anyway, here's something from a standing bar once. Talking about Cyclist A, a woman who pedals faster than everyone else, Cyclist B — a perfectly average cyclist — said, "It's like she has four legs." She probably said it without thinking, but I genuinely couldn't tell what she meant by it, and wanted to sit with that a while.

For starters, every bicycle I know of only has two places to put your feet. Even if you had four legs, it's obvious two of them would just be extra. I did consider the idea that the extra two might kick off the ground for additional acceleration, but kicking the ground while already at speed would presumably require serious leg strength (do it carelessly and you'd surely just slow down). Or maybe, in her mental model, adding legs automatically comes with more pedals too. Etcetera, etcetera.

While thinking all this over, I finally arrived at one plausible conclusion: the simple theory that the extra two legs are spares, for when your main two get tired.

In which case, what Cyclist B values as essential for covering a set distance quickly isn't speed, but stamina. Put another way, it suggests that when she's tired on a bike, she routinely thinks "if only I had spare legs to swap in." It occurred to me this might be a feeling only cyclists understand, and it made me a little curious about cycling. I almost never ride a bike myself.

That's the sort of thing I was thinking about, elbows propped on the counter, tipping back a beer with my other hand. I looked like I was part of the women's conversation without actually being part of it, and I certainly wasn't going to announce, "here's what your story made me think about." I was just there, straight-faced, like a collapsed hanger rack.

I love this kind of "earnest misalignment" — the sort the "four legs" line represents. Like cherry blossoms that bloom on a warm winter day, it's something you can go on admiring forever. Deeply poetic. And it's also the lavender left out on a balcony, a cat's tongue that never quite got tucked back in, boarding the express train going the wrong direction, a bag with two folding umbrellas stuffed inside it, sleep-talking, a mobile game after its servers have shut down, and — for that matter — Shinjiro Koizumi and Eiko Kano.

The poet Sakutaro Hagiwara once wrote that, as a rule, poetry is a product of abnormal psychology, and that poetry can never be born from the common-sense arithmetic of 1+2=3; only in the rare natural disaster or upheaval that throws ordinary life into extraordinary turmoil can one leap free of common sense into the heavens. In ordinary, sensible life, he says, a person thinks of a lamp after night, and of sleep after a lamp — but people in love think of a rose after night, and of music after a rose. Their associations leap in a way that defies common sense. And that, he says, is "poetry."

In other words, it's precisely a situation's deviation from common sense that gives it its charm. But the moment you start engineering that deviation on purpose, the whole thing falls apart — at that point it's just design, and a different matter entirely. What's needed is a misalignment that arises completely naturally.

In that sense, Hagiwara had a point: falling in love really is a good thing. It's a natural move, it might make you able to write poetry, and it could someday increase the number of fields on your tax adjustment form. Either way, life moves forward — not sideways, not backward.

That said, I should probably start facing reality — like the fact that there's an office-lady type sitting next to me at the Excelsior Caffè, studying from a book about taxes, or that a couple of days ago some mysterious call about the national pension came in to my parents' house, and I still need to call it back. And honestly, when it comes to the year-end tax adjustment itself, I still haven't quite grasped what exactly gets adjusted, or how. Goddamn it.
