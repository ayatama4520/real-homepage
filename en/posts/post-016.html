Speak Like It's A Cappella — A Little Tale of Stuttering

Published 2026.03.09

#AboutMe #GladIWasHonest #Stuttering

A skilled censor was permanently stationed in my mouth, and only the sounds he approved of were ever allowed to make themselves heard in the world. Because this censor held a physiological, absolute authority, all I could do, still wanting to make sound come out of my mouth, was try every trick I could think of to get past his watchful eye. Which is to say: dressing up words as other words while keeping their meaning intact, making them mimic something else, hiding them inside something, doing whatever it took to make them look plausible enough to pass.

My dream was to become a thesaurus in human form. When I was alone, I would look words up in a dictionary, searching for ones I wouldn't stutter on.

＊　＊　＊

My stutter is the repetitive kind, like a spring storm — for instance, if I get stuck on the "ha" in "haru" (spring), it comes out as "ha, haha, haru," or "hahahahahaharu," something like that. I've always been at the mercy of a single, measly two-syllable word.

So I sometimes feel like the blocking type of stutter (the kind where you can't get a single word out at all, and just go silent) would have been somewhat better. There's a certain meditative, almost mystical quality to it — as if to say, "I am not unable to speak; I am simply choosing, deliberately, not to."

If it's impossible anyway, don't give me even a shred of hope. The checkpoint gate should be kept properly shut. Otherwise, the part of me that wants to talk sticks its head out through the gap with too much momentum, only to get stuck and go no further. Don't make me blush in front of people any more than this.

For all these reasons, I have plenty of reasons, big and small, to dislike spring — and even though sublingual immunotherapy can cure hay fever, there's no such cure for stuttering, so naturally, I just dislike it, plain and simple. For someone who can't properly introduce himself, entrance ceremonies, class reassignments, cherry-blossom viewings full of strangers, job interviews, first-day-at-work greetings, exchanging business cards — all of it turns into something like a witch hunt, an inquisition, a fumi-e trial, some kind of device built to root out social otherness. Goddammit.

＊　＊　＊

As a child, I think I was overflowing with the sheer determination to end conversations as fast as possible. I have the feeling that I disposed of conversations the way you'd pick flowers before they bloom, mutter "sorry," and toss them alone into an incinerator. I have the feeling I gave vague nods and hums like "uh-huh," the way you might when talking to someone from another country.

Just like that mean kid never let me into the secret base he built on the hill behind the park, I don't think I ever let anyone step into the core of who I was, either.

It was around the upper years of elementary school that I first had a proper conversation with the censor. I was sitting alone on the raised step at the entrance of my grandmother's house, with the lights off, and from somewhere high above, out of my reach, an ordinary daytime light was streaming in.

"If you put something like 'uh,' 'ah,' or 'um' in front, I'll let it through. For 'haru,' you could go, 'uh, haru,' 'ah, haru,' 'um, haru,'" he told me. "I see!" I thought. If "ha" won't come out, I could just tack on a different sound first. (For what it's worth, that kind of conversational run-up is apparently called a "filler" — I only learned the term as an adult.) In the end, the censor turned out to be surprisingly flexible, and from that day on I somehow became the kind of person who always looks deep in thought, always seemingly pensive.

＊　＊　＊

While pretending to think it over with a drawn-out "uhhhhhh," count one, two, three, and say "haru" on four. In other words: let me speak like it's a cappella. Come to think of it, I never stutter when I'm singing.

＊　＊　＊

When I want to say, "this spring..." I don't need to reword it as

"this season this year..." (which season, exactly)
"the cherry-blossom season this year..." (that has a certain elegance to it, I'll admit)
"this year's spring-u..." (Lou Oshiba style)

— none of that is necessary anymore!

Substitutions like that only really work within a goofy conversation among goofy friends — where you need enough trust for someone to laugh and say, "what's with that phrasing, just talk normally," or, even without saying anything, at least find it amusing and think, "yeah, this guy's a weirdo, as expected." In a formal, official conversation, all you get is being written off as an actually weird person, plain and simple.

＊　＊　＊

Kiyoshi Shigematsu's "Kiyoshiko," Shuzo Oshimi's "Shino Can't Say Her Own Name," Yukio Mishima's "The Temple of the Golden Pavilion" — none of these are, in fact, my scripture. They don't hold up my heart.

The protagonists of those stories are all stutterers, all of them pitiable, all of them absurd, and for that, all of them dear to me — but of course, none of them are me, and in fact, my stutter isn't nearly as severe as theirs. I caught sight of the version of myself who, looking at things like that, thought, "ah, well, at least I'm better off than this person," standing off under the dim light of some distant streetlamp. I kept my distance from that version of myself going forward.

In the middle of university entrance exam season, my homeroom teacher told me, "books you read during a rough time like this stay with you for the rest of your life, so read something, whatever it is." Figuring maybe that was true, I went to a bookstore. Maybe because I was sick of looking at red exam-prep books, I found myself favoring books with blue covers. I was the only one reading a book in the cram school's self-study room, and I thought, serves you all right.

It was during that morbid stretch of time that I casually picked up Yu Kono's "Vanish, Ultramarine." I fell for a girl in it named Hori. Or rather, "fell for" isn't quite right — she was, essentially, me. She's such a peculiar character that she can't hold a proper conversation because she overthinks every word she chooses, and instead writes her replies as letters after the fact — but for me, well, I thought, yeah, that really is how it should be done, if we're being honest. What was written there was as plain and obvious as something out of a moral-education textbook, and I found myself thinking, yeah, yeah, sure, while tears ran down my face. Even if the things you want to say increase, there's always a limit to the things you can actually say. Whether you stutter or not doesn't really make much difference. It's just that the range is a little wider, that's all.

＊　＊　＊

In the end, it was only in the past two or three years that I realized it's suspicion — the fear that I won't be understood correctly, that I won't be heard, that I'll be rejected — that accelerates my stutter. Repetitive blocks, hard blocks, fillers — none of that actually matters at all. It was, from the start, purely a matter of my own soul. And after that, the censor apparently started slacking off on the job quite a bit, established a work-life balance all his own, and settled into a comfortable, leisurely life.
