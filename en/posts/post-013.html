Physical Exam: Too Much Deep Breathing Makes Me Sleepy.

Published 2026.02.28

#Essay #AboutMe #HowIFeelRightNow #Hayfever #PhysicalExam #HearingTest

"Yesterday there were 104 traffic accidents; fortunately, no fatalities," the police box announces, and I pass in front of it on my way to a physical exam.

In the park along the river, runners in colorful outfits are out in full bloom. A shower of light falls from overhead, lighting up cherry blossoms at about fifty percent bloom — beautiful. Beyond the railing, spring is already just about here.

＊　＊　＊

At the hospital I meet all sorts of people, and yet all I ever say is "yes," "no," or "Tamaoka" — which I find funny. Even though this checkup is entirely for my own sake, I'm laughing at how utterly lacking in agency I am within it. Whenever I'm involved with anything official like this, I'm always like that.

So, as at least some small act of resistance, I want to try saying something to the nurse during the blood draw — "warm today, isn't it," or "what did you eat yesterday?" — but I figure that would probably get in the way of her actual job, so I give up on the idea.

"This might feel a bit cold," the nurse says, and while I'm thinking, "what will?", a piece of alcohol-soaked cotton brushes the crook of my elbow. In the end, I stayed silent all the way through three vials of blood being drawn.

＊　＊　＊

I want someone to grip my upper arm with about the same firmness as a blood pressure cuff.

＊　＊　＊

Being told, "empty your pockets," probably only happens when you're getting mugged, questioned by police, or at a physical exam. Instantly, I get anxious about whether there's something embarrassing in there.

Into the white basket go the contents of my pockets — cigarettes (American Spirit 1mm), a lighter (pink BIC), keys (with far too many keychains attached, given how few actual keys there are) — tossed in casually. Of course I'm the one doing the tossing, but I'm deliberately acting careless about it, or maybe unconscious of it, playing it off like it's none of my business — so I'll go ahead and write it that way here too.

I'm putting on the air of, "it's not like I feel any shame at all about you seeing what's in my pockets."

Of course, this is exactly the kind of moment when I'm not carrying a paperback with me. I happen to be reading Yasunari Kawabata's "The Sound of the Mountain" these days, so if that had, say, appeared out of my back pocket, the nurse might have thought I had a certain kind of taste, and the conversation might have moved on to "actually, I read that one too," and maybe, at least, we could have become something like ordinary friends.

＊　＊　＊

If you make me take deep breaths that much, I'm going to get sleepy, so please go easy on it.

＊　＊　＊

This is a completely pointless aside, but once postwar Japanese people stopped boiling water in iron pots, they became deficient in iron. That iron deficiency led to more hay fever sufferers. More hay fever sufferers meant more masks got sold. Masks sap people's vitality, which is how men's eyes lost their vigor, and the lower half of women's faces lost their color (their makeup).

And then some of the more severe hay fever sufferers, despite not being underwater at all, put on goggles and set out to swim endlessly across the sea of dry land. Opportunists, drawn in by the sight, joined them one after another, growing in number. At every crosswalk in every city, people in goggles lined up side by side, waiting for the light to change. They were searching for a new kind of freedom.

Years later, when the president of a mask company, having amassed an enormous fortune, began scattering money around under the guise of a social experiment, the world collapsed as quietly as dawn breaking...

By the way, wearing a mask inside the hospital was mandatory.

＊　＊　＊

The one exception was the gray-haired doctor who handled my consultation — his pace of speech and the softness of his voice felt strangely un-institutional. Unlike the nurses, whose demeanor resembled workers inspecting collected parcels, his tone of voice carried the warmth of an actual human being.

"Anything bothering you? A cold, or anything?" he asks, and I answer, "just some hay fever, I guess." "Ah, that must be rough. Is it already flying around out there?" "Yeah, it's flying already." "Right. Well, that's everything, then. Please wait outside in the waiting area." I find myself satisfied that this was exactly the right feel for a conversation between two people meeting for the first time.

＊　＊　＊

At the reception desk, checking that no tests were missed, I notice that the hearing test — which I definitely never took — has been checked off.

"Consultation / Hearing Test (Conversational Method)"

Consultation. Hearing test. Conversational method. It turns out that consultation earlier apparently doubled as a hearing test — meaning the gray-haired doctor's distinctive way of speaking was part of the test all along. He was just another cog in the giant system too?! I'm deeply, deeply disappointed.

But wait, isn't a hearing test not supposed to be like that in the first place? I wanted to put on those headphones you only ever wear for a hearing test, hear that beeping tone you only ever hear during a hearing test, and press that button you only ever get to hold during a hearing test. Farewell.
