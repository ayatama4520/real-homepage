Because It's the Winter Solstice.

Published 2025.12.26

#Essay #AboutMe #Movies #YearEnd #WinterSolstice #Popcorn #StarsAndTheMoonAreHolesInTheSky

It was a day with absolutely nothing on the schedule, so I walked through town as if kicking up patches of sunlight. Yellow socks turn into yuzu citrus. Today is the winter solstice. No wonder it's cold.

On days off, I try to watch a movie whenever I can. A search online turned up that "Stars and the Moon Are Holes in the Sky," based on the original story by Junnosuke Yoshiyuki, was playing, so I thought, this is it, and headed out.

Everywhere I looked around Hibiya Station, where the theater I wanted was, had the same kind of texture, and it made me feel like I was walking through the inside of one enormous building — a castle, say, or a ship. TOHO Cinemas Chanter sat in a corner of that meticulously arranged area.

That theater has enough history to be called practically the original prototype of Japan's mini-theaters. The year it opened, 1987, was one giddy year: the bubble economy was at its peak, the "asa-shan" (morning shampoo) boom had arrived, and Machi Tawara's "Salad Anniversary" became a bestseller with 2.8 million copies sold.

"One for 'Stars and the Moon,' please," I said — self-conscious, as if saying the movie's full title out loud might be somehow embarrassing. "Do you have a seat preference?" asked the woman at the counter, her face sunk out of clear view behind the frosted glass. "A little in front of center, right in the middle," I said. I hadn't spoken to anyone yet that day, so I had no idea whether I was pitching my voice correctly. Was my voice really reaching her, past that geometric pattern of little round holes punched in the glass? After a brief silence, she said, "How about around here, then," and pointed to a sheet of paper with the seats drawn on it. Not digital. Only she could see how full the theater was, so all I could do was say, "Sure."

The elevator at Chanter was filled with the toasty smell of popcorn, so much so it felt almost artificial. I couldn't help but suspect someone had scooped air straight from the popcorn machine and was diligently sealing it in here. Maybe that's just a marketing tactic, or maybe it's simply corporate effort. Impressed by that kind of dedication, I decided to buy a large caramel one.

A 2,000-yen ticket and 500-yen popcorn are by no means cheap — that's enough money to buy eight used paperbacks at around 300 yen each from a secondhand bookstore, which I could then spend all the way until early spring enjoying. I'm someone who hesitates even to buy a pack of ten city-designated garbage bags for 400 yen.

The seat had no cup-holder-style dip for the popcorn. Left with no choice, I cradled it in my lap as though it were something precious, and it turned into an urn. Looking around, I saw a room of unfamiliar adults, at least a decade or more older than me, all staring fixedly in one direction, and it brought back a funeral I'd attended as a small child. There's a strange tension in a screen before the movie starts.

There's no such thing as a boring person, so there's no such thing as a boring movie — or something like that, I feel like someone once said. For the record, this movie was thoroughly enjoyable.

Is it mere nostalgia, this longing for the old-fashioned way of speaking used in "Stars and the Moon" — or rather, for the beauty of phrasing found in novels of that same era? Or was that period genuinely the peak of the Japanese language's beauty?

For what it's worth, there's a theory about one tendency — or perhaps just a stretch — in the speech patterns of that era: that men spoke from universal truth toward personal experience, while women spoke from personal experience toward universal truth. Put simply, men say "dakara" (therefore), women say "datte" (because).

For instance, a man says, "Today is the winter solstice. Therefore it's cold." A woman says, "It's cold today. Because it's the winter solstice." It doesn't really matter either way, but in any case, I love the way women spoke back then.

I've been thinking about something the actress Sakuya, who starred in "Stars and the Moon," said in an interview — that she based her way of speaking on Ayako Wakao's performance in the 1964 film "Manji," based on the original story by Junichiro Tanizaki. I'd like to watch that one too, sometime soon.
