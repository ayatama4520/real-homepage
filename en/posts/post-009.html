Fabric Softener Ruined My Sense of Salad Smell.

Published 2026.02.14

#AboutMe #Salad #FashionSense #FabricSoftener

A story from the near future. Nameless artists who keep diving into the deep, dark hole of creative work finally manage to put down their brushes for good — crushed, at last, by the creative power of AI.

At first, they hunch over like a worn-out sponge, but within a week they're heading out into the world like normal people and starting jobs; within a month, they're using their honestly earned money to make their home life a little richer (they'd only ever used detergent before, and now they go and buy fabric softener!). Within half a year, they invite someone they love over, and surely eat salad together every night — Cobb salad, Caesar salad, salad topped with baby leaf greens. And within a few years, they get married. Once a year, they take a trip to a riverside hotel or something. They get a cat. And then they live happily ever after until they die.

It's a bit like Taiyo Matsumoto's "Ping Pong," you could say. Having a transcendent talent crush an ordinary person's dream is, in its way, practically an act of kindness — which is to say, artificial intelligence might just become a light guiding humanity forward...

＊　＊　＊

Speaking of fabric softener, I bought fabric softener for the first time in my life. I'd always wondered how, exactly, people in general keep up their motivation to do laundry — and buying fabric softener turned out to be the answer.

But then, what even is fabric softener, in the first place? Having grown up in a household that hardly used the stuff, I didn't properly understand what it even was; apparently, if you open the little lid tucked in the corner of the washing machine drum, that's where you're supposed to pour it in.

The fabric softener tester I smelled at the store had a subtle, rounded scent, like a hinoki cypress bath or temple incense (agarwood, sandalwood, that sort of thing) — but once I actually used it, it gave off this sharp smell, like the bathroom at my parents' house or a hospital waiting room, and I couldn't stand it anymore. It was a smell that had become common currency specifically as a smell meant to mask other smells. Which is to say, it paradoxically ends up proving that the person using it stinks.

＊　＊　＊

I learn that fabric softener's scent has almost the same potency as perfume. There's no eating properly with this stuff on. I can't make out the delicate aroma of salad leaves at all. Anyway, it's a weekday afternoon, and I'm currently in a small neighborhood café.

At the table across from me is a young office-worker type eating a hamburger steak plate, phone in one hand. He's wearing a gray jacket and slacks with black leather shoes, and he's about the same height as me.

I can't help but almost mutter, "That's fast-fashion fabric!" — and stop myself. It's not that fast fashion is bad, exactly, but this guy, who looks like the type to think, "eh, clothes for the office can just be about this level," somehow strikes me as a small, small man.

He's the type whose eyes lose all focus the moment he takes a big bite, so taking advantage of that, I get to stare intently at his face and mannerisms. And looking closely like that, he turns out to be a good-looking young man with the kind of well-arranged features you'd find on a Johnny's (trainee).

And so he eats his hamburger steak, ketchup spattering around his mouth like sparks. The white pimple beside his nose, and the redness surrounding it, seem to tell a story about his everyday diet. His black hair, parted in the middle, might as well be a mass-produced stamp flowing down an assembly line; his thin stubble, I can't tell whether he's trying to grow it out or simply forgot to shave. On the phone in his hand, a tower defense game is, in fact, still running. His eyes, like a sleepwalker's, remain fixed on empty space.

So then, who exactly is this guy? He probably never goes to mini-theaters, and he's been to Village Vanguard exactly once, with friends, specifically for the 18+ section. He'll read Tatsuki Fujimoto, but not Natsujikei Miyazaki or Shun Umezawa. He buys secondhand clothes, but never secondhand shoes. He fills his home with appliances in whatever colors appliances typically come in. He lives with a certain pride in thinking things like, "I bet I'm someone different from everyone around me, since I catch myself wondering what would happen if I shouted at the top of my lungs in some quiet, crowded place." Somehow, it occurs to me that I might be a little bit like him, and that scares me.

But when he finishes his set meal and stands up, he turns out to be surprisingly tall. The fact that I hadn't noticed at all while he was sitting means, in other words, that it was only his sitting height that was short. The gray slacks he's wearing are, after all, too short for his astonishingly long legs, and the sock peeking through the gap by his shoes was the orange of a sunset. It had a single embroidered light-blue flower on the side, as an accent.

In truth, that alone is enough to make me feel saved. I'm seized by the urge to go tearing through a patch of midday sunlight, fast enough to leave my blood-sugar-spiked skull behind. And at the very back layer of the city's scenery, transmission towers march along a low hill! Yes — someone once set foot up there too. Humanity's fleeting yet stubborn will.
