I'll Buy You a Round.

Published 2025.12.19

#Essay #WorkTalk #LessonsLearned

It's the dead of night, the kind of hour that would silence even a crying child. In the smoking area, a blazing incandescent bulb holds back the darkness within a small, fixed radius, refusing to let it any closer. In that oddly one-sided space — like a line a kid once drew in a schoolyard — I'm talking with a young man who says he's from Korea. Tall, with narrow eyes and long legs, wearing a black leather jacket with an absurdly oversized collar and matching black flared pants: he looks like a clever, crafty crow. My line of work often has me talking with people who've come to Japan from abroad, and this is one more instance of that.

"Hey, I wanna take a Japanese girl home from a nightclub in Shibuya, gimme some advice," he said. I figured he'd picked the wrong guy to ask.

The most I could tell him was something like, "just hold a not-too-sweet drink in one hand, put on a meaningful face, and stand near the girls, man" — but I didn't want to seem like some standoffish jerk, so I tried to have a proper conversation about it.

As a stalling tactic, I said something like, "It's difficult, y'know. Difficult to explain, y'know." The pause fell in a place no native speaker would ever put it, because I was busy trying to remember whether the bridge from "difficult" to "explain" was "to" or "for." Right now, the left half of my brain is doing grammar, and the right half is coming up with advice on how to pick up girls.

While I was hemming and hawing, he fired off, "So should I just say 'ai shiteru' [I love you]?" I can't quite say that's the worst possible move — I've heard that, culturally, more direct expressions of affection are common where he's from, and even preferred. But does that really hold for someone you just met for the night?

For now I answered, "No, no," and then told him, "Firstly, you should say, 'Can I buy you a drink?'"

There's an order to things. Like, if you're going to eat toast for breakfast, first you put on Thelonious Monk's "I'm Confessin'," take the bread out of the bag, set it on a clean cutting board, score it in a crosshatch pattern, spread the butter on, and then put it in the toaster. For what it's worth, I prefer my bread raw, so I never actually do any of that.

"That's sick. How do you say that in Japanese?" he asked. "Ippai ogoru yo" — literally "I'll buy you a cup," though ippai can also mean "a ton" — I told him. "Ippai ogoruyo, ippai ogoruyo," he repeated, and for some reason doubled over laughing, then spread his wings and flew off somewhere. In his hand was a can of strong chuhai, gripped tight. Good luck, I thought.

＊　＊　＊

Now, tucked warmly into bed, what I find myself thinking is that, on the point of linguistic ambiguity, he's probably going to lose. Because if he — his brain already scrambled by strong chuhai — says "ippai ogoruyo" with that same momentum, in that same broken Japanese, opening his narrow eyes as wide as narrow eyes go, it could easily come across as, "I'm gonna pour drinks down your throat like a waterfall, so brace yourself."

Which is all to say, maybe I really should have just told him: "hold a not-too-sweet drink in one hand, put on a meaningful face, and stand near the girls, man."
