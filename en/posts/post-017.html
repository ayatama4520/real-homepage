Toranomon / Window Tax / Horizont

Published 2026.03.11

#AI #GenerativeAI #AboutMe #GinzaLine #MarunouchiLine #WindowTax #Horizont

The commercial for some company called Mitsubishi Jisho House Net, playing on the train's in-car monitor, was so obviously AI-made that I thought, I'm never going to rely on this company for anything, ever, in my whole life. Which is a rude thing to conclude, given I don't even really know what the company does.

Anyway, here I am, riding the Marunouchi Line, moving at high speed through the city center on a sunny day! The red Marunouchi Line cars, once they surface above ground, look wonderful under the sun.

Why the Marunouchi Line's hanging straps are round (when there are triangular straps out there in the world) is, I think, something you don't need to ask anyone about — a little thought should get you there. But then again, who's even wondering about that in the first place?

While I'm at it, I also think about why everyone's wearing outerwear of the same color and shape, who actually looks at the ads on the pillars at the station, and when on earth the construction at Yotsuya Station is ever going to end. And also why I'm the only one wearing a hat in this train car. But well, either way, I've already worked out the answers in my own head. Again, no need to ask anyone.

＊　＊　＊

I transfer to the Ginza Line at Akasaka-mitsuke. I get off the Ginza Line at Toranomon. Walking near Toranomon Station, a lifetime's worth of windows line up in rows and columns. Gleaming with the reflected light of early afternoon, they tower into the sky like a horizont light rig, staging every passerby as the protagonist of their own life.

＊　＊　＊

Speaking of windows, apparently there used to be something called a Window Tax in Europe, long ago. Governments trying to efficiently collect taxes from the wealthy reasoned, "a house with lots of windows means rich people live there!" — and taxed households based on how many windows they had (window glass, at the time, was a luxury good).

In order to save on taxes, people gamely bricked up their own windows. As a result, the government effectively stole wind and sunlight from its own citizens. In the stagnant air of these houses' interiors, all manner of disease spread. Truly, this radical-sounding policy ended in utter failure. And apparently, as a remnant of that era, there are still buildings standing today with bricks stuffed tightly into the hollows where windows used to be.

This is exactly why history is great! Everyone, desperately, doing the wrong thing — it's the best. I'd love to become one of those interesting people myself, someday. Don't you think so too? You secretly think that too, don't you, really? Then this is no time to be having a conversation with the average. Or, then again — maybe that's exactly the kind of thing that becomes a funny story, centuries down the line......

Ah, whatever, I guess. A dip in the ground, visible only on rainy days, is calling out to me. In a life with no correct answers, what am I even so tense about?
