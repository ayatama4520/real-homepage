New Year. Kazuo Umezu's House Is Always Red and White.

Published 2026.01.03

#Essay #AboutMe #NoteNewYearWriting #Couples #NewYearHoliday #BlackTea #RowBoats #FirstShrineVisit #Kichijoji #InokashiraPark

0:12 a.m., just after the new year began. A moon as hazy as a blanched daikon radish quietly watches over what we're all up to. The red lights of ambulances and fire trucks race busily by, lighting up every white wall in town. Even though it's the new year, someone somewhere has collapsed. The empty sake bottles and barrels piled high behind a closed izakaya prove that last year and this year are, in fact, one continuous stretch of ground. When on earth is the enormous Uniqlo in front of the station going to turn its lights off? Even though it's the new year.

The local shrine, which had been practically deserted just a few years ago, is this year swarmed with a wave of people. Back then it was probably just the lingering effects of COVID, so that couldn't be helped — but even so, I couldn't hide my surprise at how much things had changed. I found myself worrying that the shrine's guardian deity might be thinking, "what a convenient bunch of people." And I get the feeling the deity has something to say about the fact that the long line snaking around the shrine grounds is made up entirely of couples with a drink in hand, or small mixed-gender gangs of delinquent teens — but I decided to stop thinking about it any further. Once I stopped thinking about it, the guardian deity seemed to stop thinking about it too.

And so, having given up on visiting the shrine with my usual guardian deity that I go to every year, I'm heading instead toward a small temple to the south of town, a red structure floating on a pond. The deity enshrined there, Benzaiten, is — as you may already know — a goddess, the sole woman among the Seven Lucky Gods. Because of this, there's a folk belief that if a couple rides the rowboats on this pond together, she'll get jealous and split them up. But honestly, rowing a boat is quite a lot of work, so it wouldn't be at all strange for a low-durability couple to break up over it regardless — and that would be the fault of the man who decided to get on the boat in the first place, not Benzaiten's.

First of all, I'd like you to imagine just how grueling it is to have to keep rowing a boat non-stop for thirty minutes or so. I've ridden pedal boats plenty of times, so I know: it's like climbing a staircase that only ever goes up. I say "only ever goes up" because the trip back is never any easier. The pond runs roughly 750 meters end to end, but if you deliberately — with no real need to — steer along the overgrown, tree-branch-heavy edges of the pond (and mysteriously, most people end up wanting to do exactly that), you're probably in for a round trip of over a kilometer. Terrifying.

Now, about fifteen minutes in, the conversation dries up completely. Both of them are, quite simply, tired. At this point, in the man's head, two options are weighed against each other: honestly say, "I'm a little tired, should we turn back now?" — or put up a front and say something like, "it'd be a waste, let's go all the way to the far end." But the man picks the latter. Because he's utterly bound up in the pride of having decided to get on the boat in the first place, along with a sense of responsibility to make sure the woman is enjoying herself. It's not as if going all the way to the far end of the pond is actually going to be fun for her — cutting it short and going to buy some ice cream instead would be perfectly fine too — but there's no way a man on a boat is ever going to think of that.

While thinking things like that, you crash into another boat sailing nearby (also, generally, carrying a couple). This is by now almost a foregone conclusion — not something you can avoid once you've chosen the timeline where you got on a boat in the first place — and there's nothing to do but accept it as fact. That said, the issue isn't the surface-level embarrassment of having crashed; what's really — unwillingly — being tested and judged here is the man's split-second ability to respond when a total stranger accidentally intrudes on a space meant for just the two of you. Even if you go "Ah, sorry, sorry" and hurriedly pedal and steer, a boat simply can't change direction that fast, so you end up putting your own clumsiness on display in front of the woman for a good while longer. And of course, it's always exactly at these moments that the man in the other boat happens to be perfectly calm, which gives the women something to compare the two of you by. That said, staying unbothered and nonchalant would be strange too — since you're inconveniencing someone else, you'd better show at least some visible effort to sort things out quickly. And yet giving the other man crisp, businesslike, almost systematic instructions like, "I'll back up first, and in the meantime you steer right and pass through — right meaning your right" is unsettling in its own way. It's the same kind of fear you get watching your partner's voice go strangely high-pitched and fast when they're on the phone booking a restaurant, going "yes, yes, uh-huh, uh-huh, understood" in an overly obedient rhythm. I called it a test of split-second responsiveness, but honestly, thinking it through, the moment the collision happens you're already cornered on every side — no matter what you do from there, the relationship between the two of you cools off, if only a little.

＊　＊　＊

The white security lights installed at the entrance of every house in the quiet residential neighborhood switch on one after another as I walk past, and it makes me feel like I'm walking a runway. Getting my mood lifted by something this trivial probably means I'm somewhat fortunate as a person, and even once the Kohaku Uta Gassen New Year's music show has ended, Kazuo Umezu's old house remains, as ever, striped in red and white.

I stop by a 7-Eleven I happen to pass. It's already playing music that uses koto and shakuhachi (something like "Haru no Umi"), and imagining the conversation the staff must have had just before the switch — "Once it hits midnight, we're changing to New Year's-type music, okay?" "Oh, really?" "Yeah, that's just how it's operated." "Got it." "Make sure to actually tell me, okay?" "Got it. Oh, wait, the date already changed." "Hey—" "Happy New Year" — settles something calm in my heart.

After hesitating over whether to buy alcohol, I buy a bottle of Gogo no Kocha (Afternoon Tea) black tea instead, and leave the store. I place total, unwavering trust in black tea's antibacterial effects, and drink it nearly every day. It's essentially a self-induced placebo effect at this point — self-suggestion, a kind of prayer or charm — something science can't explain, and yet it's definitely true. Which is to say, if I ever stopped drinking black tea, I'd surely come down with the flu immediately. For what it's worth, among the different teas, I like black tea more than green tea or oolong tea, but it wasn't until the age of twenty-seven that I first learned they all come from the very same tea leaf (the tea plant). This year, I need to study up on a lot more practical knowledge like that.
